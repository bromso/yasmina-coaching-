*The conversation below is invented but typical. It is built from situations many managers will recognise, and the people in it do not exist.*

Many managers know what they want to say in a hard conversation and get stuck on how to say it. So I have written one out and annotated it line by line. The lines are my suggestions, drawn from many years of conversations as a family therapist and as a manager. No research has tested these exact words, so treat them as craft.

Here is the situation. Karin is a unit manager. Erik has been a social worker for six years, skilled and well liked by the families he works with. His case notes have been behind for more than a month, and at the last team meeting he interrupted a new colleague twice, in a way that made her go quiet.

## The opening

**Karin:** Thanks for coming in. I want to talk to you about two things, the case notes and Tuesday's team meeting. I'll be direct.

*My note:* Karin names the subject in her first sentence. Opening with small talk or praise leaves the other person waiting for whatever is coming. Naming the subject straight away is a form of respect.

**Erik:** Okay. Sounds serious.

**Karin:** It matters to me. And I want to hear how you see it.

*My note:* She promises to listen, and she doesn't pretend it is a small thing.

## The first subject

**Karin:** The case notes first. When I looked last week, four cases were missing notes going back to the start of last month. That means your colleagues can't pick up a case if you're away, and the families have a right to an accurate record.

*My note:* She describes what she has seen, with a time frame and a number, and what it leads to. She says nothing about what kind of person Erik is. "You're sloppy" is something to defend against. "Four cases have no notes since the start of last month" is something to act on.

**Erik:** I know. I haven't had time. I've had three assessments that needed home visits every week, and I put the families before the computer.

**Karin:** I understand that. You do good work with the families, I hear that from several directions. And the notes still need to be up to date.

*My note:* There is a trap here. It is easy to say "but" and wipe out the praise. Karin says "and". Both things are true at once, and she lets them stand side by side. The appreciation is real. [A study that followed 208 employees at two points in time](https://doi.org/10.1037/str0000111) found that interruptions at work took less of a toll on the well-being of those who felt appreciated by their supervisor.

**Karin:** What would you need to catch up?

**Erik:** Honestly, a day without home visits. Maybe two.

*My note:* Karin asks instead of dictating a solution, since Erik knows best where his time goes. Researchers call it autonomy support when a manager gives people a say in how they do their work. [A review of 72 studies](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11031-018-9698-y) found that employees who experienced that kind of support were more often driven by their own motivation and reported better well-being. The links say nothing about cause, but it is reason enough to ask first.

**Karin:** Let's do that. Thursday and Friday next week you book no visits, and I'll take your urgent calls. We'll go through the notes together on Monday week.

*My note:* She closes the subject with something concrete: days, who does what, and a time to follow up.

## The second subject, the harder one

**Karin:** Then the team meeting. When Amira was talking about her idea for the group sessions, you interrupted her twice. After that she didn't say anything else in the meeting. I don't think you meant anything by it. I still want you to know how it looked from where I sat.

*My note:* This one is harder because it is about how he treats a colleague. Karin sticks to what she saw and what happened next. She says what she believes about his intention and keeps it apart from the effect. That makes it easier for Erik to listen without getting defensive.

**Erik:** I thought she was wrong. We tried that three years ago and it didn't work.

**Karin:** That may well be true, and I want you to say so. Next time I'd like you to wait until she's finished, and say it then. You have experience the team needs. I also want the new people to dare to say what they think.

*My note:* This is where Karin sets the boundary. She says exactly what she wants him to do, and why. He can still disagree, and say so. What she is protecting is everyone's room to speak. Amy Edmondson calls it psychological safety, and in [her study of 51 work teams](https://doi.org/10.2307/2666999) in one manufacturing company, that safety went with how much the teams engaged in learning, and that in turn with how well they performed. The study is from 1999 and another industry, so I borrow the concept and little else.

**Erik:** Okay. I can talk to her as well.

**Karin:** I think she'd appreciate that.

## The close

**Karin:** One more thing. I raised this because I want you to stay here for a long time. How does it feel now?

**Erik:** A bit sour, if I'm honest. But it was fair.

*My note:* Karin asks how it feels and takes the answer as it comes. She doesn't try to make him feel good before he leaves. A hard conversation is allowed to feel a bit sour afterwards. What matters is that the relationship holds, and I believe it holds more often when the manager has been direct and warm at the same time.

## What I hope you take with you

Karin does four things. She names the subject at once. She describes what she has seen rather than what she thinks of the person. She asks what is needed. And she sets a time to follow up.

Respect also goes with how people feel at work. In [two survey studies](https://doi.org/10.1097/jom.0000000000001782), recognition from supervisors and co-workers was linked to less burnout, and respect was most clearly linked to lower emotional exhaustion. These are associations, and I read them as a reminder of how much tone matters.
